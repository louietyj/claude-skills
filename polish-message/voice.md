# Louie's writing voice

Drawn from about ten years of his sent Gmail (2017–2026): support tickets,
refund disputes, lawyers, clinics, recruiters, landlords, professors,
teammates, friends. Since about 2025, his longer emails often start as
Claude drafts that he then restructures and heavily compacts. Those sent
versions show where he lands after editing, but the earlier mail is the
better guide to how he writes from scratch.

## How to use this

- **The examples are paraphrased from real emails, to show shape and
  register.** Don't reuse their wording. A phrase that fit one situation
  reads like parody when it shows up in another.
- **Don't stack features.** A typical message uses a handful of these
  traits, picked by register. A dentist gets a crisp question and
  `Thanks!`. A teammate might also get a `:)`. Nobody gets a `:)`, a
  `--`, a casual aside, and a bolded sentence all in one email.
- **Situational sections apply only to that kind of message:** working
  with collaborators, raising a concern, announcing to a group,
  declining, pushing back.

## In one line

Brief, direct, informal, and precise: says what he wants in the first
sentence or two, backs it with exact facts, and stops.

## Openings

- **Greeting by register.** `Hello,` to a support inbox or a stranger
  with no name. `Hi <Name>,` once there's a name. `Hey <Name>,` for people
  he's already talked to or wants to sound warm with. `Hi all,` to a
  group. `Hello!` for a friendly vendor.
- **Quick notes put the greeting inline:** `Hi Sam, here are the documents
  for all 3 of us.`
- **Replies within an ongoing thread usually skip the greeting** and start
  with the substance: `I think …`, `Ah ok, in that case …`, `Actually,
  …`, `Ok!`, `Done.`
- **Then straight to the point.** No "I hope this email finds you well",
  no "I'm reaching out because". The first sentence is the situation or
  the ask: `We booked a 12pm tour on 7 Mar (Booking ID ABC-123, $482.04)
  which was described as a 3h tour.`
- **Replies open with thanks for the specific thing:** `Thanks for meeting
  me today!` / `Thanks for escalating the ticket!`

## Closings

- **Usually `Thanks!`** on its own line, sometimes `Thanks a lot!` or
  `Thanks so much!`. Never "Best regards", "Warm regards", "Cheers",
  "Sincerely". (`Regards,` was his default through about 2019 and then
  dropped out. Don't bring it back.)
- **Collaborator replies and firm emails often have no sign-off.** The
  last sentence is the point, the position, or a question like `What do
  you think?`
- **His Gmail signature is added automatically.** Never type a name or
  signature into the draft.
- **A short forward-looking line before the thanks is fine:** `See you
  tomorrow!` / `Let me know if there's anything else you need from me.`

## Body

- **Short.** A confirmation is one sentence. A request is one short
  paragraph. Emails only get longer when there are several questions or a
  case to make, and then the length goes into structure, not prose.
- **Numbered lists for multiple questions,** introduced with `A few
  questions:` or `My questions:`. One question per item. Bullets for
  supporting points or grounds.
- **Exact facts up front, inline:** booking, ticket and claim numbers,
  amounts to the cent, dates, links, and which card or account. The
  recipient should never have to ask "which one?".
- **States his understanding and asks for confirmation** instead of asking
  an open-ended question: `I believe I'd still pay the contracted rate,
  just out of pocket, right?` / `To make sure we're on the same page, this
  was the proposal: …`
- **Answers the obvious next question in advance:** `Happy to do any other
  identity verification if necessary.` / `No worries if that's not an
  option, phone still works.` / `If not, send me some times and I'll do my
  best to move things around.`
- **Says why when it matters, briefly:** `This is sort of time sensitive
  since I have to send the old phone in by Oct 15.` One clause, not a
  backstory.
- **Bolds the one sentence that must not be missed,** if there is one:
  the key ask, the instruction, or the line he's holding. Never more than
  one or two per email.

## Asks

- Phrased as questions, politely, without grovelling: `Could you check on
  this?` / `Do you mind double-checking …?` / `Would you mind holding off
  on X until …?` / `Any chance we could …?` / `Any ETA?`
- `Just` softens follow-ups and check-ins: `Just following up on this.` /
  `Just checking if you were able to run it?`
- Follow-ups are short and point to what was already promised: `Following
  up on <ticket> again. Any ETA? Per your 23 Sep email, …`

## With collaborators

This is how he writes to a supervisor, teammates, a lawyer he's working
with, or anyone he's solving a problem alongside:

- **Disagrees directly and says why.** `I don't think X. We want Y, not Z.`
  / `I'm confused, why are we doing X if Y?` He doesn't soften it into a
  suggestion.
- **Makes the point concrete** with a specific case rather than an
  abstract argument: `Say a student has a deadline extension on one
  assignment. The page would suggest only that one can ever change.`
- **Gives options and asks for a decision:** `Either we do A or we do B?`
  / `I'm not sure what the best approach is. What do you think?`
- **Lays out how he's framing the problem,** concedes the fair objections,
  and lands on a position while staying open to a better one: `Here's how
  I think about it: … I get the concerns though: … So let's go with X.`
- **Very short turns are normal:** `Done.` / `Updated. The important change
  is on page 8.` / `As promised it's really one line: <link>`

## Raising a concern or making a case

- **Lead with the concern:** `I'm concerned about whether I'll be able to
  meet the timeline.`
- **Then evidence, with numbers and links:** `the PR has 50-odd broken
  tests and each kind of failure is taking 4+ hours to track down`, plus
  the link.
- **Frank about where things stand,** including his own shortfalls: `Most
  of what's done so far is conceptually simple.` / `I'm sure it's
  possible given more time, but at the current rate it's going to be
  hard.` No spin.
- **Long cases get structure:** numbered reasons with short bold headings,
  then a bold **Proposed solution**.
- **End by asking for a decision or a discussion,** not with a summary.

## Announcing to a group

- **The one thing people must do or not do goes at the top,** bolded:
  `**tldr: Don't edit this page unless you know what you're doing. Ping me
  if you think you need to.**`
- **Short bold section headers** (`What it is`, `When you should change
  it`) with brief paragraphs or numbered cases under each.
- **Assignments and dates as bullet lists.** The meeting time is bolded.

## Declining or deciding

- **A gracious decline:** thanks for the specific thing, the decision in
  one sentence, the reason in one more, and the door left open. `Thanks
  for hosting me through the process. After much consideration, I've
  decided to accept another offer. … Hope we can stay in touch.`
- **With someone he knows, the real reason, lightly:** `having to pay back
  the signing bonus before I've even started is not very palatable :)`
- **Closing something out:** `After some consideration, I don't think
  it's worth pursuing since A and B. I'll absorb the cost. Thanks so much
  for your input!`

## When he's pushing back

Disputes and refusals are their own mode, and he's good at it:

- **Grounds as a bulleted list,** each one a concrete fact.
- **When he has the receipts, a dated record:** `This was the agreed-upon
  behavior, consistent with:` followed by a bullet for each meeting, doc,
  or draft with its date. Only with real dates he gave you. Never invent
  them.
- **Acknowledge their side first, then hold the line:** `I fully
  understand that logistical issues happen, but because the tour was
  materially shorter than advertised …`
- **Propose the specific remedy with a number:** `A 30% refund ($160.68)
  feels fair, based on getting about 2h instead of the advertised 3h.`
- **Sparing but real emphasis:** one word in CAPS (`it was NOT
  disclosed`) or one bolded sentence (`**Please stop contacting me.**`).
- **No sign-off and no pleasantries at the end.** Firm and polite, never
  sarcastic or threatening. His student-era firm emails were blunter
  ("Please do not make things up."). Draft to the current, more measured
  style.

## Tone and texture

- **US-facing English only. Never Singlish or Singapore-English idioms**
  (`lah`, `wah`, `can I check if …`, `anyhow`, `shiong`), even for
  Singaporean recipients.
- **Informal but spelled out.** Contractions everywhere (`I'm`, `we'd`,
  `that's`). But **never** `pls`, `thx`, `ty`, `u`, `ur`, `rn`, `lmk`,
  `np`: always `please`, `thanks`, `let me know`, `no worries`. Domain
  abbreviations are fine (`ETA`, `fyi`, `tldr`, `e.g.`, `PR`).
- **Text emoticons, not emoji:** `:)` most often, `:P` when playful, `:(`
  or `:/` for mild disappointment. Only where the register allows, at
  most one per message, usually at the end of a sentence.
- **Exclamation marks on thanks and greetings** (`Thanks!`, `Hello!`,
  `See you tomorrow!`), rarely elsewhere.
- **`--` (double hyphen) as a dash,** with spaces: `I'd love to chat --
  but in full transparency, …`. Not a typographic em dash.
- **Plain, conversational phrasing:** `so we can screen share and stuff`,
  `this is sort of time sensitive`. Occasionally a self-aware aside in
  parentheses that comes from the situation at hand, at most once per
  email.
- **Apologises briefly and moves on:** `Sorry for the late response.` /
  `Sorry, forgot to attach!`
- **Dates:** `7 Mar 2026` or `23 Sep` in prose. Times like `12pm` or
  `2:30pm`.
- **With friends, even shorter and playful:** `Hey, scanned letter for you
  as requested.` His Gmail has few of these messages, so keep it simple
  rather than guessing at a style.

## What he doesn't sound like

If a draft contains any of these, it isn't in his voice yet:

- "I hope this email finds you well" / "I hope you're doing well" /
  "I'm reaching out to …" / "I wanted to follow up on …"
- "Please don't hesitate to …" / "Please feel free to …" / "At your
  earliest convenience" / "I look forward to hearing from you"
- "Best regards," / "Warm regards," / "Kind regards," / "Regards," /
  "Cheers,"
- "I would be grateful if …" / "I was wondering if perhaps …". He just
  asks.
- Restating the recipient's email back to them before answering.
- A closing paragraph that summarizes what the email already said.
- Em dashes, semicolon-heavy sentences, "Additionally," / "Furthermore," /
  "Moreover,".
- A long backstory before the ask.
- Stacked hedges: "I just wanted to quickly check whether it might be
  possible …".
- Softening a real disagreement into "perhaps we could consider …".
